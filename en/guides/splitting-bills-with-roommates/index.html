How to split bills with roommates

Sharing a place cuts costs but adds decisions: who pays what, how to split rent when rooms aren't equal, and what to do about shared groceries.

By Valentin, creator of %BRAND% · Updated October 24, 2026 · 3 min read

Separate three kinds of costs

Start by agreeing on what's shared and what isn't. Almost every household cost fits one of three groups:

  • Fixed: rent, building fees, internet. Known in advance and paid every month.
  • Shared variable: electricity, gas, water, cleaning supplies, shared groceries.
  • Individual: each person's own food and personal purchases. These stay out of the shared account.

It sounds obvious, but most conflicts come from things nobody classified: toilet paper, cooking oil, dish soap, the streaming subscription "everyone uses".

Rent: equal shares?

If the rooms are similar, splitting evenly is simplest. If they aren't, adjust. Common criteria:

  • Room size: whoever has the biggest room pays a bit more.
  • Private bathroom or balcony: these add to a room's value.
  • Number of people: a couple sharing a room uses more common space and more utilities.
  • Light and noise: a room facing a busy street or without a window may be worth less.

A practical approach: set a percentage per room at the start and keep it as long as you live together, for example 40/30/30 when one room has its own bathroom. What matters is that it's written down and everyone accepts it before moving in.

Utilities and groceries

Utilities are usually split evenly. If someone works from home all day and runs the heating or AC much more, it can make sense to adjust, but don't overcomplicate it.

For groceries there are two models:

  1. Everything shared: you shop together and split the bill. Works when eating habits are similar and you often eat together.
  2. Only the basics shared: cleaning supplies, toilet paper, oil, salt, coffee. Everyone buys their own food. The most stable model when schedules and diets differ.

Who pays, and when to settle

The tidiest approach: whoever can pays at the moment, logs it, and once a month you settle everything at once. Nobody has to front all the costs or chase small amounts after every purchase.

With a shared record, the end-of-month math is automatic: everyone sees what they paid, their share and who to pay. To make transfers quick, each person adds their payment details (bank details, PayPal, Venmo handle) once.

When someone moves out

It's the most delicate moment of living together. Settle these in advance:

  • The deposit: if one person paid it, it goes back to them; if everyone chipped in, split it by what each put in.
  • Pending debts: settle up on moving day so the person leaving is square.
  • Things bought together (a microwave, a fridge): whoever stays can buy out the leaver's share, or you sell and split.
  • Utilities in the leaver's name: transfer them before they go.

Rules that prevent conflict

  • Write down how each kind of cost is split.
  • Revisit the agreement when something changes: a new roommate, a bill goes up, someone starts working from home.
  • Settle every month: debts that pile up are the ones that end badly.
  • If someone can't pay one month, talk about it then, don't let it slide.

FAQ

What about guests who stay for days?

Short visits usually cost nothing. If someone's partner is around half the week, it's reasonable for that roommate to chip in a bit more for utilities and groceries.

Should we open a joint bank account?

It can help for rent, but it adds paperwork and complicates things when someone moves out. For most households a shared record and monthly transfers are enough.

Tip: the same method you'd use for a trip works for a shared home. Create a group for the apartment, log the month's expenses and do a partial settle-up at the end of each month: settled expenses stay in the history and the next month starts from zero.
