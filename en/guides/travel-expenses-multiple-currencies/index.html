How to handle travel expenses in multiple currencies

Traveling abroad adds a layer of complexity: you pay in the local currency, sometimes in dollars or euros, and you settle debts in your home currency. Sorting it out is simpler than it looks.

By Valentin, creator of %BRAND% · Updated October 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Three currencies on one trip

Take a typical case: four friends from Chicago spend a week in Lisbon. The apartment was booked from home and charged in euros. In Portugal they pay almost everything in euros, but a day trip booked through a US site was charged in dollars. And when they get home, they'll pay each other back in dollars.

Three roles, often three currencies:

  • The trip currency (euros): where most expenses happen and where it's best to keep the books.
  • Occasional currencies: one-off payments in something else.
  • The settle-up currency (dollars): what the group uses to pay each other back.

Mixing them up causes almost every error. The trick is to keep them separate, with three rules.

Rule 1: log each expense in the currency you paid in

If dinner was paid in euros, log it in euros. If the day trip was charged in dollars, log it in dollars. Converting in your head on the spot introduces errors and mixes exchange rates from different days.

The practical setup is a tool that defaults to the destination's currency, so 90 % of expenses need no thought, and lets you pick another currency when needed.

Rule 2: convert other-currency expenses at that day's rate

When an expense is in a different currency from the trip, convert it to the trip currency at the rate on the day it happened, and lock that rate. That way the expense's value doesn't change if the currency moves next week.

Example: the day trip cost 150 dollars and that day 1 dollar was worth 0.92 euros. It's recorded as 138 euros. If the dollar drops by the end of the trip, that expense is still 138 euros: that's what it actually cost.

In TribuSplit, you tap "Other" when adding an expense, pick the currency and the app fetches the day's rate. If the payer knows their card charged something different, they can correct it.

Rule 3: agree on the currency you'll settle in

The currency you spend in and the one you settle in are separate decisions. Americans in Lisbon spend euros and settle in dollars; Canadians in Mexico spend pesos and settle in Canadian dollars; a mixed group from the UK and Germany might spend in Japanese yen and settle in euros.

The procedure that avoids errors is always the same:

  1. Work out all balances in the trip currency.
  2. Work out the minimum transfers in that same currency.
  3. Only then convert each transfer to the settle-up currency, using a single agreed rate.

Converting at the end, and only the transfers, keeps every intermediate calculation exact and applies the rate exactly once.

Which rate should you settle at?

There's no single "correct" rate. Several references are reasonable:

ReferenceWhat it isWhen it fits
Market rateThe mid-market rate published by exchange services and central banksA neutral default when nobody has a reason for another
Card rateWhat the bank actually charged, including feesWhen whoever fronted the money paid mostly by card
Exchange office rateWhat you got changing cashWhen the trip was mostly paid in cash

The question that settles it: how much did it really cost, in their own currency, the person who fronted the money? A lower rate than their real cost means they lose; a higher one means they gain at others' expense. For most trips with fee-free cards, the difference between market and card rates is small, and the market rate is the simplest choice.

Common mistakes

  • Adding different currencies without converting. 100 euros plus 50 dollars isn't 150 of anything.
  • Converting everything at the last day's rate, even though expenses happened over weeks.
  • Choosing the rate at the end, when everyone already knows which one suits them. Agree beforehand.
  • Ignoring card and ATM fees: if they're significant, they can justify using the card rate. More in paying abroad without hidden fees.

FAQ

What if everyone lives in a different country?

Pick one common settle-up currency (often dollars or euros) and let each person convert from there with their own payment method. You can also run separate splits in different currencies, but it usually complicates more than it helps.

Can we change the settle-up currency mid-trip?

Yes. Each split, partial or final, has its own currency and rate. You could settle a partial split in reais while in Brazil and the final one in dollars back home.

In short: log in the currency you paid in, lock the rate for other-currency expenses, calculate everything in the trip currency, and convert only the final transfers at a rate you agreed on in advance.
