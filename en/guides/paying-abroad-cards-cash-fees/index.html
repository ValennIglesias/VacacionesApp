Paying abroad: cards, cash and how to avoid hidden fees

The exchange rate you see online is rarely the one you get. Between card fees, ATM charges and bad conversions, a trip can quietly cost several percent more than it should.

Updated October 20, 2026 · 3 min read

Where the money actually goes

When you pay in another currency, there are up to three layers of cost on top of the price:

  • The exchange rate itself: the rate your card network or bank applies, which is usually close to the market rate but not identical.
  • Foreign transaction fees: a percentage many banks add to every purchase in another currency. It is often somewhere around 1 to 3 %, and some cards charge nothing at all. Check your card's fee schedule before you leave.
  • ATM fees: your bank may charge for withdrawals abroad, and the local ATM operator can add its own fixed fee on top.

None of these show up on the receipt, which is why they are easy to miss.

Always pay in the local currency

Card terminals and ATMs abroad often offer to charge you "in your home currency". This is called dynamic currency conversion, and the rate it uses is usually worse than the one your own bank would apply. The rule is simple: when asked, choose the local currency. Your bank does the conversion, typically at a better rate.

The same goes for ATMs: if the screen offers to "lock in" a conversion rate for you, decline and continue in the local currency.

For most trips, the best combination is a card without foreign transaction fees for most purchases and a small amount of cash for markets, tips and places that don't take cards.

Getting cash without overpaying

  • Withdraw less often, in larger amounts, if your bank charges a fixed fee per withdrawal.
  • Use bank ATMs rather than standalone machines in tourist areas, which tend to charge more.
  • Avoid airport exchange counters for anything beyond what you need to get to your lodging.
  • Carry a backup card from a different bank in case one gets blocked.

What this means when you split costs with a group

When several people pay in different ways (one with a card, another with cash exchanged at the airport) each of them effectively paid a slightly different rate. Trying to track each person's exact rate gets complicated fast. Two simpler approaches work well:

  1. Log every expense in the local currency, the amount on the receipt. That keeps the shared record exact and fair.
  2. Agree on one exchange rate when you settle up, such as the rate on the day you settle or the average of what people actually paid.

Before you leave: a quick check

  • Look up your card's foreign transaction fee and ATM fees abroad.
  • Make sure your card works internationally and that your bank's app lets you lock or unlock it.
  • Note the approximate exchange rate so you can spot a bad deal.
  • Decide with your group which currency you will use to pay each other back.
Rule of thumb: pay in the local currency, use a card without foreign fees when you can, and keep cash for the small stuff. Then log everything in one place so splitting is easy.
